Why Philadelphia's Small Business
Tax Exemption Was Eliminated

A plain-language explainer: what happened and how the LIFT Act fixes it

1

What Was the $100K BIRT Exemption?

Philadelphia has a tax called BIRT — the Business Income and Receipts Tax. Every business that does work in Philadelphia owes it, based on how much money comes in and how much profit is made.

For years, Philadelphia had a protection in place: if your business earned less than $100,000 in gross receipts, you owed nothing. Zero. You didn't even have to file. About 75,000 small businesses (freelancers, therapists, food cart operators, gig workers, barbers, consultants) were fully covered by this exemption.

In plain terms If you ran a small business making under $100K, BIRT wasn't your problem. Until 2026.
2

Why Did the City Eliminate It?

In 2024, a Massachusetts medical device company called Zoll Medical Corporation filed a lawsuit against the city. Their argument: the $100K exemption was unconstitutional.

Pennsylvania's constitution includes something called the "Uniformity Clause." It says that all taxpayers in the same class must be taxed at the same rate — you can't create a tiered system where some pay nothing and others pay full rate. Zoll argued the exemption did exactly that.

The legal problem Under the exemption, a business earning $50K paid nothing. A business earning $1M paid the full rate, but only on the amount over $100K. That made the effective rate different for different businesses. Pennsylvania's constitution says that's not allowed.

In 2017, the Pennsylvania Supreme Court had already struck down a similar tiered tax structure in another case. Philadelphia's Law Department reviewed the situation and concluded the city would likely lose in court.

Rather than wait for a court ruling that could force the city to repay years of tax revenue to corporations, Mayor Cherelle Parker signed the exemption into elimination on June 13, 2025. Effective tax year 2025.

3

What Does This Mean for Small Businesses?

Starting with 2025 tax returns (due April 2026), every Philadelphia business must file a BIRT return, regardless of how much they earn. The people hardest hit are the smallest:

75,000 small businesses now paying BIRT for the first time
$1,208+ new annual tax for a contractor earning $50K
$4,600+ new annual tax for a business with $95K in net profit

One Philadelphia therapist reported a BIRT bill over $5,700 for 2025 alone. That's on top of the Net Profits Tax they already pay. Freelancers, gig workers, food cart vendors, and solo practitioners across the city are facing the same shock.

The double taxation problem Solo business owners pay both BIRT and the Net Profits Tax. Large corporations like Comcast do not face this same double burden. The BIRT elimination made that inequity dramatically worse for Philly's smallest operators.
4

How Does the LIFT Act Fix It?

The LIFT Act takes a different approach: a new legal structure built to be constitutionally durable.

The Old Approach (struck down)

Exempted businesses based on income level. This created different effective tax rates within the same class of taxpayer, which is exactly what the Uniformity Clause prohibits.

The LIFT Act Approach

Creates an entirely new tax classification for sole proprietors and single-member LLCs. Every business in this new class gets the same treatment: zero BIRT. Same rate for everyone in the class = constitutional.

Sole proprietors and single-member LLCs would still pay the Net Profits Tax, without the double-taxation of BIRT on top.

Confirmed Constitutional on the Record

At an April 2026 City Council hearing, Frances Beckley, Chief Counsel of the Philadelphia Law Department, stated on the record: "I would confirm that the bill as written does satisfy the uniformity clause." That's the city's own attorney, on the record, before City Council.

No New Paperwork. For Anyone.

Philadelphia's BIRT and Net Profits Tax forms already ask filers to check a box identifying their business type: individual, partnership, or corporation. The LIFT Act uses that existing checkbox to determine who qualifies. The city's revenue system doesn't need new forms, new processes, or new infrastructure. Taxpayers don't fill out anything extra. The mechanism is already built in.

Councilmember Mike Driscoll introduced the bill (Philadelphia City Council Bill 251026) on November 25, 2025, backed by the LIFT Philly coalition representing more than 75,000 individual-owner business entities across every zip code, industry, and category.

What About the "Loophole" Concern?

The remaining objection raised by some city officials is this: if sole proprietors and single-member LLCs pay no BIRT, won't wealthy S-Corp and C-Corp owners simply restructure as LLCs to escape the tax?

The math says no. S-Corps save roughly 15% in federal self-employment taxes, savings that have nothing to do with Philadelphia's tax code and doesn't change if you switch to an LLC. That federal savings overwhelmingly outweighs any BIRT benefit from restructuring, at virtually every income level.

On top of that, converting from an S-Corp to a sole proprietorship costs $4,000 to $11,000 in legal and administrative fees and triggers a 5-year IRS lockout before the owner could re-elect S-Corp status. No rational business owner with a real accounting team recommends this.
